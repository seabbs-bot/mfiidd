Particle filters: propagate, weight, resample

A stochastic model gives a different trajectory every run, so the likelihood of a parameter set is an average over the paths the epidemic could have taken. A particle filter estimates that average by simulating paths, weighting each one against the day's count, and keeping the ones the data support. This page runs that filter on the Tristan da Cunha data, one day at a time, with the SEIT4L model of the practical.

What to look for

On a day in the first wave the particle cloud is wide, because the epidemic could still have taken off at very different speeds, and the count is large enough that the Poisson weight falls away quickly on either side of it. A handful of particles take almost all the weight and the rest are dropped, so the distinct-particles count falls to a small number. That is the filter working, not failing.

With resampling off, the effective sample size falls day after day and does not recover, because weights multiply. Within a week or so one particle holds nearly all the weight, and the estimate is then built from a single trajectory.

With 8 particles the running log-likelihood moves by tens of units between runs. That is the noise the next session has to sample through, and it is why the number of particles is something to calibrate rather than guess.

What to look for

Read the count off your own run. The point is that this is measured, and measured at the parameters you intend to sample near, because the same count is noisier elsewhere in the space.

Doubling the particles halves the variance of the log-likelihood, so it cuts the standard deviation by about √2, and it doubles the time per evaluation. In particle MCMC that noise is what makes a chain stick, so the trade is time per iteration against the number of iterations needed. Aiming for a standard deviation between one and three is where the total tends to be cheapest.

The estimate of the likelihood is unbiased, and the logarithm of an unbiased estimate is biased downwards by Jensen's inequality. The bias shrinks as the variance shrinks, which is exactly the rise you see on the right-hand panel.

Common questions

Short answers, in the order they usually come up

Why is the average weight the likelihood, with nothing to correct for?

The day's factor is p(yt | y1:t−1) = ∫ p(yt | xt) p(xt | y1:t−1) dxt. Propagating the particles through the model draws them from the density on the right, so evaluating the observation density at each particle gives values of the function on the left at points already distributed correctly. Averaging them estimates the integral directly.

The estimate is unbiased, so why does the mean log-likelihood change with the number of particles?

Unbiasedness holds for the likelihood, not for its logarithm. The logarithm is concave, so the average of the logarithms sits below the logarithm of the average, and the gap grows with the variance of the estimate. Few particles therefore give a log-likelihood that is too low on average as well as noisy.

Why not simulate many trajectories and average, without any resampling?

That is a valid estimator and it is what the no-resampling line shows. It fails in practice because the weight of a whole path is a product over days, so after a week almost all of it sits on one path and the effective sample size is one. Resampling restarts each day from particles that already agree with the data.

Is the noise a bias in the posterior?

No. A particle MCMC scheme using an unbiased likelihood estimate targets the exact posterior, which is what makes the approach usable at all. The noise costs mixing rather than correctness, so a chain moves less and needs to run longer.
