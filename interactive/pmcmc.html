Particle MCMC: sampling with a likelihood you cannot compute

The particle filter returns an estimate of the likelihood, not the likelihood. Metropolis-Hastings takes that estimate at face value and still samples the exact posterior, at the price of a chain that sticks. This page runs the sampler on the Tristan da Cunha data, one iteration at a time, with the SEIT4L model and the priors of the practical.

The estimate is a draw

Two runs at the same parameters give two different acceptance probabilities

Metropolis-Hastings needs p(y | θ). The filter gives an unbiased estimate of it, which is a different number every run. Below, one fixed pair of parameters is evaluated again and again, and the acceptance probability that a fixed proposal would receive is worked out from each pair of draws.

With an exact likelihood a proposal has one acceptance probability. Here it has a distribution, and at a small particle count that distribution covers most of the interval. The sampler is not deciding between the parameters. It is deciding between two draws.

Run the sampler, one iteration at a time

Propose, evaluate, decide, and keep the estimate you accepted

Each iteration proposes a parameter set, runs the filter once to score it, and compares that score with the score stored for the current state. The stored score is the one thing that makes this different from Metropolis-Hastings, and it is where the behaviour comes from.

What to look for

At 8 particles the estimate is so noisy that an accepted state is usually one whose likelihood was over-estimated. Every honest proposal after it has to beat a number that was never the truth, so the chain sits still for tens of iterations, and the age of the stored estimate climbs with it.

More particles shrink the noise, so fewer lucky values are accepted and the plateaus get shorter. The cost per iteration rises in proportion to the particle count, which is the trade the tuning section measures.

A small proposal scale raises the acceptance rate and moves the chain nowhere. Acceptance rate on its own is not a diagnostic. What matters is distance covered per unit of computation.

How many particles

Cost per iteration is linear, and movement per iteration is not

Short chains at each particle count, with the same proposals and the same starting point. Distance covered is the mean squared jump of R0 per iteration, which counts a rejection as zero and a large accepted move as a large number.

most movement per second– its acceptance rate– SD of the log-likelihood there– its longest plateau–
Read this one with care. Squared jump per second rewards cheap iterations, and a chain that accepts one lucky value and then sits still for two hundred iterations still records a large jump when it finally moves. The plateau chip is there to show what the movement measure is hiding. The rule to follow is the one the previous session calibrated: keep the standard deviation of the log-likelihood estimate between one and three, which on this data means tens of particles rather than a handful.

Why it is still exact

A noisy likelihood, and the right posterior anyway

Write u for every random number one filter run draws, with a density that does not depend on θ. The estimate is a function L̂(θ, u) whose average over u is the likelihood. The sampler is then an ordinary Metropolis-Hastings chain on the pair (θ, u), targeting p(θ) L̂(θ, u) m(u), and the margin of that in θ is the posterior, exactly. Unbiasedness is the whole requirement. Nothing asks the estimate to be good.

The check below is empirical. Chains at a hopeless particle count and at a comfortable one are pooled separately and drawn against each other. They must agree, and the noisy one gets there far more slowly.

Common questions

The ones that come up in the practical, and one that comes up as a bug

Does the filter's noise bias my posterior?

No. As long as the filter's estimate of the likelihood is unbiased, the chain targets the exact posterior at any particle count. The noise costs efficiency, which shows up as a low acceptance rate and long plateaus, and a chain that has not run long enough will look wrong for that reason rather than from bias.

Why does my chain sit still for 300 iterations?

Because the estimate stored at the current state was a lucky draw, and it stays in the denominator until something beats it. Under the target, the stored estimate sits σ²/2 log-units above the truth on average, so plateaus lengthen quickly as the filter gets noisier. Adding particles is the fix, not adding iterations.

Should I use more particles or more iterations?

Within the target band the total cost is flat, so once the filter is calibrated the rest of the budget goes on iterations. The particle filters page covers how to measure the SD and what to aim at.

Can I recompute the likelihood estimate at the current state as well?

No, and it is a common bug because it looks like an improvement. The current state's u is part of the state, and redrawing it with no accept-reject step leaves the chain no longer invariant for the extended target. The plateaus vanish and the posterior comes out too wide, which is easy to miss because the trace looks better.

Can I fix the random seed so the filter is reproducible?

Fixing the seed inside the likelihood makes L̂ a deterministic function of θ, so the chain becomes ordinary Metropolis-Hastings for that fixed function. It is exact for the wrong target: one perturbed realisation of the posterior, with the size of the perturbation set by the filter's variance. Seed the run as a whole for reproducibility, and let each filter call draw fresh randomness.

Why robust adaptive Metropolis rather than plain Metropolis-Hastings?

With six correlated parameters, a proposal that ignores the correlation aims most of its moves in directions the posterior does not extend, and hand-tuning six variances to fix that takes several pilot runs. RAM learns the proposal covariance during warmup and scales it towards a target acceptance rate. Adaptation stops at the end of warmup, so the chain that is kept is an ordinary Metropolis chain with a well-chosen proposal.

What is particle Gibbs, and when would I want it?

Particle Gibbs runs a conditional filter that keeps one reference trajectory from the previous iteration, then updates the parameters given that trajectory. It suits models where the parameters have conditional structure worth exploiting given the path, and it avoids PMMH's stickiness because the path is updated rather than accepted or rejected wholesale. Its own failure mode is path degeneracy, which leaves the early states of the reference trajectory barely changing, and ancestor sampling is the standard repair (Lindsten et al. 2014).

Does any of this depend on it being a bootstrap filter?

No. The argument needs a non-negative, unbiased estimate of the likelihood, and auxiliary and guided filters supply one as long as the weights are correct. Better filters reduce the variance, which moves you along the cost curve, and leave the exactness argument untouched.
